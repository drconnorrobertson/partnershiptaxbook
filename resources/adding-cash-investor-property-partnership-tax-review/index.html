Adding a Cash Investor to a Property Partnership: Tax Questions Before Signing

A property owner bringing in a cash investor needs more than a revised ownership percentage. Before signing the operating agreement or moving funds, have the attorney and tax adviser describe the transaction, the use of the cash and the records that will support future allocations. This guide helps an owner commission that work without treating a capital contribution and a buyout as interchangeable.

Follow the cash to its recipient

State whether the investor pays the partnership, buys an existing owner's interest or participates in a combination of transactions. Identify any cash distributed to existing owners around the same time. Include proposed refinancing, debt changes and property transfers in the same timeline.

Section 721 provides the general nonrecognition rule for property contributions to a partnership, subject to exceptions and other applicable provisions. That rule does not mean every investor admission involving payments or debt is automatically tax-free. Ask the adviser to analyze the complete arrangement rather than one isolated transfer.

Inventory the property and partnership history

Collect the property purchase documents, improvements, depreciation schedules, current debt records and prior partnership returns. If the property was contributed earlier, preserve the original contribution records and valuation support. Tell the adviser about prior ownership changes, distributions and special basis adjustments.

Distinguish current market value from tax basis. An agreed property value used in negotiations does not itself replace the tax basis of the building. The opening records should show the relevant values and explain why they differ, rather than overwrite historical tax figures to match the new investor's valuation.

Translate the economic deal into reviewable terms

Describe the cash contribution, ownership rights, distribution priorities, additional funding obligations and expected exit. If there is a preferred return or a distribution waterfall, provide the actual draft terms. Ask the attorney and tax adviser to identify how the economic arrangement will be reflected in the agreement and tax allocations.

Section 704 governs important partnership-allocation rules, including differences between contributed property's tax basis and value. A tax allocation is not established merely by choosing a percentage in bookkeeping software. Request an explanation of any special schedules the preparer will need to maintain.

Review liabilities and near-term distributions

Provide loan agreements, guarantees, refinancing plans and proposed uses of proceeds. Ask how the new investor's admission affects the relevant liability and basis calculations for each owner. Avoid assuming that the partnership's total debt or a simple ownership percentage supplies every answer.

IRS Publication 541 describes partnership basis, liabilities and distributions. If an existing owner expects to receive cash when the investor joins, have that payment analyzed explicitly. A capital contribution followed by a distribution should not be described as harmless paperwork without considering the full facts.

Agree on the closing and first-return deliverables

  • A diagram of the parties and transaction steps, including cash recipients.
  • An opening ownership and capital schedule tied to the signed agreement.
  • A record of property tax basis, relevant values and required allocation schedules.
  • An analysis of liabilities and any contemporaneous distributions.
  • A calendar for bookkeeping changes, partner information and return preparation.
  • A list of assumptions that must be revisited if the deal terms change.

Ask who will maintain these records after closing. The transaction adviser may not be the annual return preparer. Arrange a handoff that includes the final documents, calculations and unresolved questions, not just a copy of the signed operating agreement.
