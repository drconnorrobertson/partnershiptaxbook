Section 754 Election Help: What a Buyer Should Ask the Tax Adviser to Deliver

A buyer entering a real-estate or operating partnership may hear that a Section 754 election creates a tax step-up. That phrase is not a complete scope of work. Before hiring an adviser, separate the election, the buyer's basis calculation, allocation among partnership assets and future reporting. This guide is about commissioning that work, rather than estimating savings from a purchase price alone.

First confirm what is being acquired

Give the adviser the signed or proposed agreement and an ownership chart. Buying an existing partner's interest, contributing new cash to the partnership and buying assets directly are different transactions. A redemption paid by the partnership is not automatically the same as one partner buying another's interest. Identify the legal purchaser, transfer date and whether the entity will still have more than one owner afterward.

Ask for a short written classification of the transaction before requesting a depreciation forecast. The IRS partnership publication discusses transfers, basis, distributions and partnership termination. These rules explain why the closing structure matters as much as the headline purchase price.

The election is one deliverable, not the whole project

A Section 754 election is made at partnership level. Under the ordinary election procedure, the statement accompanies the partnership's timely filed return, including extensions, for the relevant year. It generally continues for later applicable transactions and cannot simply be switched off by a new buyer. The IRS election FAQ explains the procedure and potential late-election relief.

Ask whether a valid election already exists and who will obtain and retain proof. If it was missed, request a specific relief analysis instead of assuming that adding a statement to a later return fixes it. The proposal should address applicable mandatory adjustments as well; the absence of an election does not settle every basis-adjustment question.

Require a buyer-specific calculation and asset schedule

For an eligible transfer, a Section 743(b) adjustment generally concerns the transferee partner rather than a blanket increase in every partner's deductions. The relevant computation compares the buyer's basis in the interest with the buyer's share of the partnership's adjusted basis in its property. Liability allocations, asset values and special rules can affect that computation. A capital-account number alone is not an adequate substitute.

Ask what valuation support will be used, whether an outside appraisal is needed and who is responsible for asset allocation. The Section 743 regulations govern the adjustment and its partner-specific effect; Section 755 regulations address allocation. A positive adjustment is not necessarily an immediate deduction: land, depreciable property and other assets can have different consequences.

Compare proposals using the same transaction facts

  • Election review: existing status, filing responsibility, due-date controls and any relief work.
  • Basis work: buyer acquisition basis, liability information and reconciliation to partnership records.
  • Valuation and allocation: included analysis, third-party costs and assumptions that remain provisional.
  • Annual reporting: separate adjustment schedules, depreciation or amortization treatment, and owner-preparer handoff.
  • Future events: treatment when an asset is sold, another interest transfers or the buyer later exits.

Request the fee, the documents needed before the quote is final and the cost of ongoing maintenance. A proposal limited to an election statement should not be compared as if it includes a valuation report and several years of reconciled schedules.

A fictional closing example

Suppose an investor agrees to buy an interest in an LLC owning an office building. The seller supplies a K-1, a recent property appraisal and an old depreciation report. The buyer's adviser still needs the purchase terms, liability allocations, the partnership's current asset basis and prior election records. The property appraisal alone does not establish the buyer's deductible adjustment.

The useful deliverable is a documented bridge from the closing records to the buyer-specific tax schedules, with assumptions identified. If records are missing, the adviser can explain which calculation is provisional and which filing step cannot wait. This is an illustration of process, not an actual client outcome or a savings estimate.

Prepare an implementation handoff

Before closing, ask the partnership manager and existing preparer who will maintain the new schedules and authorize any election. Obtain secure access to the relevant agreement, prior returns, depreciation detail, debt records and valuation evidence. Avoid a situation where the buyer's adviser assumes the entity preparer will file while the entity preparer assumes the buyer has handled it.

At completion, retain the election evidence, computation, asset allocation and annual schedules together. Confirm that the partner's personal preparer receives what is needed without treating the K-1 capital account as the complete basis record. Keep the original work when changing accountants.

Discuss a scoped review
