Partnership Outside Basis: Track Contributions, Distributions, Debt, and Losses

Reconcile contributions, distributions, debt changes, and losses before using a partner’s outside basis.

Published July 13, 2026 • Reviewed November 22, 2026 • AE Tax Advisors

Outside basis is the partner’s adjusted tax basis in a partnership interest. It helps determine the basis limit on losses, gain on certain distributions, and gain or loss when the interest is sold. It is a separate record from the partnership’s basis in its assets and from the capital account on Schedule K-1.

Keep a dated schedule supported by contributions, allocations, distributions, and liability records. Download the blank outside-basis review record and fictional annual reconciliation examples. These are review aids, not a substitute for the IRS worksheet or a determination that a loss is deductible.

Establish how the interest was acquired

For a contribution of money or property, the general starting point is money contributed plus the adjusted tax basis of contributed property, with applicable gain and liability adjustments. Property’s fair market value is not a general substitute for its adjusted tax basis. Record contributed debt and the resulting allocation of partnership liabilities separately so the same debt is not counted twice.

A purchased interest generally starts with cost under the applicable acquisition rules. Gifts, inheritances, and interests received for services have their own rules. Services are not simply contributed property with an automatic fair-market-value basis: capital interests, profits interests, vesting, and any compensation inclusion require separate analysis. Preserve the acquisition documents and the preparer’s conclusion.

Separate capital, outside basis, and inside basis

K-1 item L’s capital account is useful supporting information, but it does not establish the partner’s outside basis. Even tax-basis capital generally excludes the partner’s share of partnership liabilities. Acquisition differences and other adjustments can also matter. Inside basis belongs to the partnership’s assets; a partner-specific section 743(b) adjustment needs separate tracking. Do not substitute the property’s value, a capital-account balance, or a loan balance for the partner’s full basis schedule.

Reconcile increases and decreases in the right order

Use the current IRS partner basis worksheet and review event timing. In a straightforward annual reconciliation, establish beginning basis; add qualifying contributions, income including tax-exempt income, and liability increases; account for distributions and liability decreases; then apply relevant nondeductible expenses and loss items in the prescribed order. A sale, liquidation, property distribution, special allocation, or other unusual transaction needs more than this summary.

A decrease in the partner’s share of partnership liabilities is generally treated as a money distribution under section 752(b). For an ordinary money distribution, money exceeding available outside basis generally produces gain under section 731. Property distributions have their own basis rules: outside basis generally decreases by the basis assigned to the distributed property under section 732, not automatically its fair market value. Marketable securities, hot assets, contributed-property rules, and liquidations can change the analysis.

Two fictional annual basis reconciliations

Both cases assume $30,000 beginning basis, $10,000 allocated income, $5,000 cash contributed, and a separately supported $5,000 increase in the partner’s liability share: $50,000 before decreases. All amounts and liability allocations are invented and assumed valid. There is no property distribution, special election, unusual timing, or other basis item. Case B differs in its cash distribution and has no nondeductible expense.

These are arithmetic illustrations, not approved debt allocations, client outcomes, or tax savings.

Basis passing a loss does not make it currently deductible

After the section 704(d) basis limitation, at-risk, passive-activity, excess-business-loss, and item-specific rules may further limit the deduction. Nonrecourse debt can affect outside basis without necessarily increasing the amount at risk; qualified nonrecourse financing has separate requirements. Maintain distinct suspended-loss records for each limitation rather than calling every carryforward a basis suspension.

Liabilities are not allocated merely by multiplying the loan balance by an ownership percentage. Recourse allocations depend on economic risk of loss and the regulations; nonrecourse allocations have their own tiers and exceptions. Guarantees, related parties, refinancing, and partner changes require review. An unsupported debt allocation cannot establish a deduction.

Distinguish a property sale from an interest sale

If the partnership sells a property, compute the partnership’s asset-level gain using its adjusted inside basis and the applicable recapture and character rules. Allocate the resulting tax items; those items can adjust partner outside basis. Separately account for cash distributions and liability changes. Subtracting a partner’s outside basis from their share of the property’s gross sale price is not the partnership’s asset-gain calculation.

If the partner instead sells the partnership interest, determine amount realized, including applicable relief of partnership liabilities, and subtract the supported adjusted outside basis at the transfer date. Separate fictional interest-sale illustration: $40,000 cash consideration plus $10,000 liability relief gives $50,000 amount realized; subtracting $35,000 supported outside basis gives $15,000 total gain, assuming no selling costs or other adjustments. This is not a property-sale example. Section 751 can make part of an interest-sale gain ordinary; holding period, depreciation-related rules, and other facts also matter. Ordinary/capital portions and final tax are deliberately unresolved here.

Build the record before a distribution or transfer

  1. Document the acquisition method and supported starting basis, rather than using value or the K-1 capital balance.
  2. Reconcile annual K-1 items, contributions, distributions, and liability shares to source documents.
  3. Identify property distributions, elections, transfers, and other events requiring a separate calculation.
  4. Track basis-stage and later suspended losses separately, and obtain the missing facts before claiming a current deduction.
  5. For a sale, identify whether assets or the interest are sold and complete amount-realized and character schedules accordingly.
